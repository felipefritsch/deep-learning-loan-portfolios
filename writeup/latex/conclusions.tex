\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

\section{Summary of findings}
\label{sec:concl-findings}

This dissertation set out to test the central claim of
\citet{sirignano2021} --- that the conditional transition distribution
\eqref{eq:object} depends on the covariates nonlinearly and
interactively --- on a cleaner, prime conforming testbed and across
regimes the original sample did not reach. The loan-level answer is
unambiguous. On a strictly out-of-sample rolling backtest over the
eleven test years 2015--2025, comprising 84.5 million frozen test
loan-months, the deep transition model beats both the covariate-free
empirical floor and the multinomial logit in \emph{every} window and
\emph{every} origin state. Pooled, the network lowers negative
log-likelihood by $6.3\%$ over the logit and $9.0\%$ over the floor;
since the logit itself recovers only $2.9\%$ over the floor, the
nonlinearity supplies roughly two-thirds of the achievable
improvement. One structural difference from the paper emerges
immediately: the architecture search at full scale selects \emph{three}
hidden layers rather than the five preferred on the subprime-heavy
private-label data --- the nonlinearity premium is real, but in prime
credit it is structurally simpler.

The network's edge concentrates exactly where the exploratory evidence
of Chapter~\ref{chap:data} located the curvature. The largest single
gain is prepayment: the \emph{current}$\to$\emph{prepaid} discrimination
rises from an AUC of $0.65$ under the logit to $0.75$ under the network,
the refinancing S-curve that a linear-in-incentive form cannot bend to.
The second is the sparse, highly nonlinear delinquency transitions the
logit cannot even rank --- \emph{dpd\_90plus}$\to$\emph{foreclosure}
goes from an AUC of $0.48$ (below chance) to $0.62$. In the delinquent
origin states the linear model sits at or below the covariate-free
floor and the network is the only estimator that beats it, so the value
of the covariates there is inseparable from modelling them
nonlinearly.

Because the comparison is run on a rolling, expanding-window design
rather than a single split, its output is itself a finding: a time
series of the value of nonlinearity. The ordering
$\text{empirical} < \text{logit} < \text{single network} \le
\text{ensemble}$ holds in every regime --- the calm late-cycle years,
the 2020 COVID forbearance window (by far the hardest, with likelihoods
roughly twice the calm-year level for all models), and the 2022--23
rate shock. The deep model's \emph{relative} edge over the logit
survives even the COVID distribution shift that no model fully
anticipates.

\section{From likelihood to price: the pool-level translation}
\label{sec:concl-pool}

The dissertation's main contribution beyond replication is to carry the
comparison from log-likelihood into the units an investor in mortgage
pools actually prices. Composing the frozen monthly models into
twelve-month horizon predictions and running each pool's predicted
prepayment-speed path through a pass-through cashflow engine yields
errors in conditional prepayment rate (CPR), weighted-average life, and
price. The headline here is not a flat ``nonlinearity wins'' but
\emph{shock-type dependence}: which model prices a pool best depends on
whether the shock lands in a channel the covariates encode. Under the
rate and prepayment-incentive shock of 2022--24 --- precisely the
channel the rate-incentive covariates capture --- the covariate-free
empirical matrix is catastrophic (characteristic-bucket prepaid $R^2$
of $-4.6$ and $-1.5$) and the ensemble is strongest, cutting the
logit's mean absolute price error by $31\%$ and $48\%$. The 2020
forbearance shock inverts the picture: the assumption-free empirical
floor is the most robust estimator and the ensemble is the worst,
because it bakes in hardest a pre-COVID prepayment signal that March
2020 abruptly invalidates (a price-error change of $-1\%$ against the
logit). These results are reported per anchor and never pooled: a
single pooled number would average the COVID inversion into a
misleading flat improvement, which is exactly the wrong conclusion. The
aggregate magnitudes are economically modest --- fractions of a point
per 100 of face, as expected when the discount curve is held fixed and
pools diversify idiosyncratic timing --- so the contribution lies in
\emph{where} the mispricing concentrates (the high-incentive cells of
the bucket-level error map in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}), not in the
pooled level.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:concl-limits}

The roll-forward freezes the entire macroeconomic block --- rate
incentive, unemployment, house-price-derived features --- at the anchor
date, so every pool-level prediction is conditional on the $t_0$
environment and there is no macro forecasting. This is a deliberate,
assumption-light choice, but it has a boundary, and the COVID anchor is
built to expose it: at December 2019 every model under-predicts the
realised $33\%$ CPR by about 17 percentage points, blind to the
March-2020 rate collapse and refinancing wave that no $t_0$ covariate
could anticipate. Four further limitations bound the reading of the
results. The macroeconomic covariates are drawn from final-revision
series rather than real-time vintages, so the backtest inherits a small
amount of revision look-ahead in the macro block. Unemployment enters
at state rather than local granularity, coarser than the loan's actual
geography. Credit terminations enter the cashflow engine's first pass
as zero-recovery events, with a recovery haircut left as an unrun
sensitivity. And the width sweep that confirms the inherited network
widths leave no accuracy on the table is a dev-scale completeness check
generalised to the full-scale deployed network. None of these
overturns the loan-level ranking, which is stable across all eleven
windows; each is a point a careful reader could otherwise press.

\section{Future work}
\label{sec:concl-future}

Two extensions follow directly. The first, deferred deliberately, is
the portfolio-decile sort of the original paper --- ranking pools and
forming best/worst portfolios. A sort tests only the \emph{ranking} of
the same per-loan probabilities, which is already tested upstream by the
AUC discrimination and the reliability diagrams, whereas the pricing
exercise tests their \emph{levels}, which is what a valuation consumes;
a sort would therefore duplicate the information in the bucket-level
mispricing map without the dollar magnitudes. The second, and the more
substantial, is to attach macro scenario paths to the roll-forward,
relaxing the frozen-$t_0$ assumption of Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.
This would convert the 2020 inversion from a boundary condition into a
testable forecast and turn the regime-conditional value of nonlinearity
documented here into an operational stress test --- the natural next
step for a model whose central empirical message is that the worth of
flexibility is itself a function of the regime.
